RCA Pilot full walkthrough: voiceover to read (about 8.8 minutes)

Read at your normal slow pace. Say the scene number, read the lines, pause 2 seconds, then the next scene. If you stumble, repeat the line.

Scene 1: The situation (39 s)
Ten to three on a Tuesday morning. The overland conveyor at the mine trips on high gearbox oil temperature. The supervisor rings the on-call fitter, who is on site by half past three. By daylight the high-speed bearing in the drive gearbox is in pieces, and the stockpile feed has stopped. Eighteen hours later the belt runs again. And it is the fourth time this year. At the production meeting the general manager asks two questions. Why does this keep happening? And what is it costing us?

Scene 2: What we are trying to achieve (39 s)
We want four things. Find the real cause, not just the broken part. Put a dollar figure on the loss, so the fix can compete for budget. Fix it so it stays fixed. And prove it, months later, with evidence. I will do it in RCA Pilot, from a blank page. The numbers are illustrative, made up to show the method. Four failures a year, eighteen hours each, nine hundred tonnes an hour, twelve dollars margin a tonne, and thirty-eight thousand dollars to repair the gearbox.

Scene 3: Sign in and start (27 s)
Go to w w w dot r c a pilot dot com. Demo mode needs no account. I will create an account with an email and password, so my work is kept safely and is there on any device. The AI buttons I use today need you signed in. This is the dashboard, empty. I click New investigation.

Scene 4: Problem: draft from notes (50 s)
Step one is Problem. At the top is a box called Draft problem statement from notes. Last time the fields were already filled, which was confusing. Today everything is empty. A technician would jot rough notes like these. Now I click Draft problem statement from notes. The AI turns the notes into a clear statement, using only what I wrote. It does not guess a cause, and it lists what the notes left out, under Not in your notes, worth finding out. I can edit the draft, then I press Use this draft. It fills the Describe the problem fields below, and I can undo it. Then I read it and correct anything.

Scene 5: Problem: asset details (18 s)
Below that, Asset details. Tag, type, make and model, duty, criticality, and the failure mode in one phrase. The tag goes into your asset register, and the failure mode links this to similar failures on other assets.

Scene 6: Loss (57 s)
Step two, Loss. If you have no figures yet, choose Not known yet, or enter one rough yearly estimate. A rough number beats none. I enter the five figures from the start of the video, and the calculation runs on the right. Eighteen hours at nine hundred is sixteen thousand, two hundred tonnes. At twelve dollars, that is one hundred and ninety-four thousand, four hundred. Add the repair, and one failure costs two hundred and thirty-two thousand, four hundred. Four a year is nine hundred and twenty-nine thousand, six hundred. The repair is cash. The lost margin is opportunity. Then, what happened to the lost production? Here it was lost for good. Made it up on overtime? Use Made up later. Stock covered it? Use Covered by buffer stock.

Scene 7: Evidence (37 s)
Step three, Evidence. Click Add evidence. Each item has a title, a type, a date, and a note on what it shows. A photo of the failed bearing. The oil temperature trend. The inspection of the oil cooler. The oil sample. The maintenance history. And the interviews. Evidence before opinions. Keep the failed parts, and photograph them before anything is cleaned up. Later I link each item to the cause it supports. That is how a theory becomes a proven cause.

Scene 8: Cause tree: by hand (52 s)
Step four, the Cause tree. The top box is the failure event. I click Why? and type the answer in plain words. The gearbox oil overheated. Why? The oil cooler fins were blocked with dust. That is physical. Why was it blocked? The cooler was not cleaned. That is a human cause. And why was it not cleaned? Because it is on nobody's task list. That is the system level, the latent cause, and I tick This is a root cause. I do two more branches the same way: dust in the oil, and misalignment after the motor change. For each cause I tick the evidence that supports it, and mark it Verified or Ruled out.

Scene 9: Natural and forced deterioration, views (29 s)
One question I always ask. Is this wear natural, or forced? Bearings wear out eventually. That is natural, managed with monitoring and planned replacement. A bearing that dies early in hot, dirty oil is forced deterioration, wear we caused. Fix the cause and it goes away. I set that under Type of wear. The same tree also shows as 5 Whys, or a Fishbone.

Scene 10: Cause coach and Challenge (45 s)
Now the AI help. The Cause coach suggests causes I may have missed, each as a hypothesis, with what would confirm it and what would rule it out. I click Suggest causes, read each one, and choose Add to tree or Dismiss. I add one that is new to me. I dismiss belt over-tension, because the motor current shows no overload. Then Challenge my analysis. It questions the tree like an experienced reviewer, from Fix first to Worth a look. It says one root cause has no evidence linked, so I link the maintenance history. The AI suggests. I decide.

Scene 11: Actions (46 s)
Step five, Actions. Corrective actions fix this failure. Preventive actions stop it happening again, one for each root cause, with an owner, due date and cost. For the cooler, the category is Maintenance task: clean the cooler fins every four weeks, by a mechanical fitter. For the oil, Condition monitoring: an oil sample every three months. For alignment, a Procedure or standard. And the spares check shows we hold no spare pinion and bearing set, with a sixteen week lead time, so I add a Spares holding action. These flow into Maintenance plan changes, ready for the planner. Total cost, fifty thousand dollars.

Scene 12: CAPA suggestions and quality check (23 s)
For each root cause there is also a Suggested actions button. It proposes corrective and preventive actions, says whether the wear looks natural or forced, and nothing is added until I click Add to actions. The Quality check lists any gaps, like a root cause without an action.

Scene 13: Verify (47 s)
Step six, Prove it stopped. Savings only count once every action is Done, the review date is entered, and the failure has not recurred. Months later I mark the actions Done and enter the review date. Recurred? No. Expected events per year after the fix? One, a cautious number. The loss falls to two hundred and thirty-two thousand, four hundred. The saving is six hundred and ninety-seven thousand, two hundred dollars, verified. Payback on fifty thousand is about four weeks, and fifty-four hours of production came back. Then Roll this fix out: the sister gearboxes each get a check, with an owner and a date.

Scene 14: Report (23 s)
Step seven, Report. Draft report writes the full report from everything I have entered. Management summary, one page, is what the general manager reads. I can tell it who the audience is. Then I edit the text and check every fact. When it is right, Print or Save as PDF.

Scene 15: Close (24 s)
That is the whole method. Describe the problem. Size the loss. Gather evidence. Ask why, down to the system. Fix it with owners and dates. Prove it stopped, and spread the fix. Try it at w w w dot r c a pilot dot com. The link is in the description.
