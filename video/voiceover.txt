Scene 1. Think about the last breakdown that made you drop everything. The call in the middle of the night. Crews pulled off planned work. The line standing still. Now ask a harder question. Was it the first time? Most of us have fixed the same pump, the same gearbox, the same conveyor more times than we care to count. Every repair feels like progress. It is not. It is a bill. And we keep paying it. This video is about how to stop it. Find the real cause, fix it, and prove it stays fixed.

Scene 2. Let me tell you about one I lived through. We had a bucket conveyor feeding our packaging lines. In one year it failed about four times. Each time, production stopped, and packaging stopped with it. And we had no spare buckets. None on site. So we stripped the least worn buckets out of the damaged sets and patched the conveyor back together, just to get running. The new buckets had to be made to order, then air freighted in. That meant more cost and more waiting. And until they arrived, we were running on salvaged parts, with the lines exposed to the next failure. Four times in one year. Same conveyor. Same scramble. Every time, we got it running. But getting it running is not the same as fixing it.

Scene 3. Root cause analysis, or RCA, is a structured way to answer one question. Why did this really happen? Not what broke. Why was it able to break? We trace a failure back through the chain of causes until we reach something we can change, so it cannot happen again. That last part matters. A good RCA ends with a change to the plant, to a procedure, or to the way we work. If nothing changes, nothing was analysed. It was only discussed.

Scene 4. So let me say what RCA is not. It is not a hunt for someone to blame. The moment people feel blamed, they stop sharing facts, and facts are all we have. It is not stopping at the first cause. The bearing failed because it overheated. True, but that is where the investigation starts. And it is not replacing the part and calling it fixed. A new bearing treats the symptom. The conditions that killed the old one are still there, waiting for the new one.

Scene 5. Why does this matter for the plant? Because firefighting is expensive in ways that never show on one budget line. Crews are tied up on emergency work, so planned work slips, and that sets up the next failure. When repeat breakdowns stop, the plant runs without interruption. The cost to produce or process each unit falls, because the same fixed costs are spread over more good output. Profit rises. And you get there without large spending. No new equipment. No major project.

Scene 6. I call this the hidden plant. Every site already owns capacity it does not get to use. It disappears an hour here and ten hours there, to failures that keep coming back. Add up those lost hours across your critical equipment and you can find a whole extra line's worth of output. It is already built. Already staffed. Already paid for. Fixing repeat failures releases it. That is capacity without large capital spending, or CAPEX, which simply means money spent on new assets.

Scene 8. So what does an effective RCA look like? It has six parts. One, a clear problem statement, with the loss in numbers. Hours, tonnes, dollars. If you cannot size the loss, you cannot judge the fix. Two, evidence before opinions. Photos of failed parts, trend data, maintenance history, operating logs. Collect it before anything is cleaned up or thrown away. Three, a cause and effect tree. Start with the problem and keep asking why. In branches, not one straight line.

Scene 9. The tree goes down through three layers. Physical causes, the part or the condition. Human causes, the decisions and actions. And system causes, also called latent causes. Those are hidden weaknesses in procedures, standards and management that let the human error happen. Four, check every cause against evidence. If you cannot verify it, it stays a theory. Five, actions with an owner and a date. Six, a check afterwards that the failure really stopped coming back. Now let me show you how this works on a typical problem.

Scene 10. This worked example uses illustrative numbers. I made them up to show the method, so please do not treat them as benchmarks. Picture a slurry pump feeding a process line. No standby pump. No buffer tank. When it stops, the line stops. In twelve months it has failed six times. Bearings and mechanical seals. A mechanical seal is the device that keeps the slurry inside the pump where the shaft passes through the casing. Each failure stops the line for fourteen hours.

Scene 11. Now put the loss in numbers. The line runs at one hundred and twenty tonnes an hour. Margin is eighteen dollars a tonne. Fourteen hours times one hundred and twenty is sixteen hundred and eighty tonnes. Times eighteen dollars is thirty thousand, two hundred and forty dollars. Add nine thousand, five hundred dollars of repair cost. That is thirty-nine thousand, seven hundred and forty dollars a failure. Six failures is two hundred and thirty-eight thousand, four hundred and forty dollars a year.

Scene 12. Next, evidence, before opinions. We keep the failed parts. The seal faces are scored and heat cracked, which tells us they ran dry. The bearing races show wear tracks that are off centre, which points to misalignment. Oil samples show water and solids. The control system shows flow at about sixty percent of the pump's best efficiency flow. That is the point where the pump runs smoothest. And the maintenance system shows no alignment records for four of the six rebuilds.

Scene 13. Now we build the tree, level by level. Why did the seals and bearings fail early? Physical causes first. One, the pump runs far from its best point, so the shaft is pushed sideways every turn. Two, poor alignment after rebuilds, including a soft foot. That means one foot of the pump base is not flat, so tightening the bolts bends the frame. Three, contaminated bearing oil. Four, a restricted seal flush, the clean liquid that cools and protects the seal faces. Then the human level. Operators throttle the discharge valve. Fitters align by straight edge. Hoses wash down onto the bearings. Nobody checks the flush strainer.

Scene 14. Now ask why again. This is the system level, and this is where the roots are. Nobody reviewed the pump duty when throughput changed. There is no precision alignment standard. The rebuild procedure has gaps. There is no verification after repair. Flush and oil condition are on no inspection route. And failures are logged only as bearing failed, so the pattern stays hidden. Fix these, and people are no longer set up to fail.

Scene 15. Before we act, we test each cause against the evidence. Off its best point? The flow trend shows it. Poor alignment? The records are missing. A cause the evidence cannot support gets dropped. Then the actions. Slow the pump down with a variable speed drive, so it runs near its best point with the valve wide open. Fit a flush flow indicator and add it to the inspection route. Set an alignment standard, buy a laser tool and train the fitters. Seal the bearing housings, and add a rebuild checklist with a vibration reading before handover. Each has an owner and a date. Total cost, sixty thousand dollars.

Scene 16. Twelve months later, the pump has not had a single breakdown. Not one. The yearly loss of two hundred and thirty-eight thousand, four hundred and forty dollars is gone. Sixty thousand divided by that saving is about a quarter of a year. Payback is about three months. And eighty-four hours of production came back. That is ten thousand and eighty tonnes of capacity, from a pump we already owned. Now, bearings and seals still wear. That is natural deterioration, and no analysis removes it. What we removed was forced deterioration, the wear we were causing ourselves. The natural wear is now watched with vibration, oil and flush checks, and the parts are changed in a planned shutdown, before they fail.

Scene 17. Here is what I want you to take from this. RCA is not an event after a big failure. It is a system. A repeatable routine. Set clear triggers, like repeat failures or hours lost. Use the same steps every time. Track every action to closure. And check, at ninety days and again at twelve months, that the failure has gone. Do that consistently and firefighting fades. The plant gets quieter, and the hidden plant appears. The hard part is doing it on paper. Trees, evidence and follow-up get lost in spreadsheets and email. So I am building a tool to make this easy, and I will share more soon. Until then, pick one repeat failure at your site and run the six steps. Thanks for watching.
