## 1. The 4am call (the leaking packs story)

It's 4am on a Thursday. The night shift on Line 3 does its 2-hourly seal check.

The operator pulls 3 packs off the end of the line and drops them in the leak tester. 2 of them leak along the seal.

Everything packed since the last good check goes on hold. About 6,000 packs.

Then the phones start. The production manager at home. Then QA.

A retailer had already complained about slack packs last month. This was the 6th time this year.

I knew what the GM would ask. Why does this keep happening, and what is it costing us?

Most sites can answer the first one with a guess. Almost nobody can answer the second one with a number.

## 2. Why RCAs die in a list of "we should"

Most RCAs I've seen end the same way.

A page. A cause tree if we were lucky. And a list:

We should check the seal bar temperature.
We should retrain the operators.
We should review the PM.

Nobody owns it. No date. No check later. Three months on, the failure is back and the RCA is in a folder.

A list of "we should" is not a countermeasure. A countermeasure has an owner, a date, and a test that says it worked.

What does your site do with the action list after the meeting?

## 3. Put a dollar figure on the repeat failure

Before you can get a budget, you need a number.

Try this on your worst repeat failure. Rough is fine.

Events a year. Hours of line time lost each time. Output per hour. Margin on that output. Then the cash costs: call-out, scrap, product on hold, claims, rework.

In the example I use (made-up figures, 6 events a year at 8 hours each), one failure comes to about $263,000 a year. It was never one big cost. It was lots of small ones that nobody added up.

Once you have the number, the argument about whether it is worth acting on is over. The argument becomes which countermeasure.

## 4. "Done" is not a result

A seal fault stops on Tuesday. Someone writes "done" on the board.

It comes back in March.

I learned to ask a different question: how will we know this countermeasure worked, and when will we look?

Pick the measure before you start. Pick the date. Put it in the diary. If the failure has not come back in [90 days / 6 months / one full production cycle], close it. If it has, it was not the root cause. Go back to the tree.

Savings count after that check. Not before.

Do you go back and check?

## 5. Natural or forced deterioration?

When something fails, one question saves a lot of time.

Was it worn out the way it should wear? Or was it pushed to fail early?

A bearing at the end of its life is natural deterioration. The plan may be fine.

A bearing that lasted 3 months because of misalignment, lubrication or overload is forced deterioration. Changing the interval won't help. You have to find what forced it.

Mix the two up, and you will change the PM and keep losing bearings.

I've seen both. What did you find last time?

## 6. Evidence beats opinion in the RCA room

In every RCA meeting someone says "it's the operators" and someone says "it's the maintenance".

Both are opinions.

The rule I tried to stick to: no cause goes on the tree without the evidence next to it. A trend, a photo, a work order history, a measurement.

If you can't find evidence, it goes on a list of things to find out. Not on the tree.

It makes meetings shorter. It also makes it harder to blame a person when the system caused it.

## 7. What I learned running plants

I've been a Maintenance Manager, a Production Manager and a Site Manager.

The same lesson from each seat: the repeat failures that hurt most are rarely the big dramatic ones. They're the small ones that happen every month, and everyone has stopped looking at.

Production sees lost hours. Maintenance sees parts and labour. QA sees complaints. Finance sees none of it until the year-end.

Put them in one number. Then the conversation changes.

## 8. Roll it out to the other lines

You find the cause on Line 3. You put in the countermeasure. It works.

Then what about Lines 1, 2 and 4?

If the same sealer, the same product or the same pump is on the other lines, it can fail the same way. The learning is worth little if it stays on one line.

Ask the question at the end of every RCA: where else could this happen? Then list the assets and give each an owner.

It costs very little. It's the cheapest saving in the whole investigation.

## 9. Retailer complaints are an RCA trigger

A complaint from a retailer is not just a quality problem. It is a signal that you've been losing money for a while.

If you have a slack pack complaint, you probably also had holds, scrap and rework before it.

When the next complaint comes in, try this: before the CAPA form, work out the annual cost of the issue. Then decide how much effort it deserves.

Most sites I knew would have spent more.
